**Binary:** `prob` (PIE, NX, canary, full RELRO, IBT/SHSTK)
**Constraint:** shellcode may only use a very small seccomp-approved syscall set
**Goal:** recover a flag that the program hides, encrypts, and then wipes

## What the program does

The process reads `./flag` into a page at

```text
((rand() & 0x3fffff) + 0x100000000) << 12
```

It derives a 32-byte AES-256-GCM-SIV key from the low byte of the next 32 `rand()` calls, encrypts the flag, copies the Base64 result back to the hidden page, wipes the key and heap ciphertext, and then executes user shellcode.

After the seccomp filter is installed, only `mmap`, `munmap`, `exit_group`, and a restricted `write` remain useful. A write is accepted only when its buffer is on `0x31339000` and its length is at most eight bytes.

## Locating the hidden page

The shellcode probes candidate pages with `mmap(MAP_FIXED_NOREPLACE)`. A successful mapping means the half-page is free; `EEXIST` means the existing hidden mapping is in that half. It immediately unmaps successful probes and binary-searches the 4,194,304 possible page indexes in 22 probes.

Once the page is found, the shellcode copies eight bytes at a time to the permitted write page and emits 256 bytes. The first eight bytes disclose the page address, so the Python side recovers `rand() & 0x3fffff`.

## Recovering the key

The seed is only 24 bits. `solve.py` brute-forces seeds whose first `rand()` output has the leaked low 22 bits, reconstructs the next 32 key bytes, and tests each candidate with AES-GCM-SIV. The binary passes a null IV; its 12-byte random update is authenticated data, so decryption uses a zero nonce and the recovered AAD.

```python
flag = AESGCMSIV(key).decrypt(b"\0" * 12, ciphertext + tag, aad)
```

The shellcode builder and the seed search are in [solve.py](https://github.com/peterlim11123-lab/boroctf/blob/master/peekaboo/solve.py). The page-search reasoning is also recorded in the challenge's [exploit notes](https://github.com/peterlim11123-lab/boroctf/blob/master/peekaboo/exploit_notes.md).

The interesting lesson is that the crypto is not attacked directly: a restricted memory-disclosure primitive leaks exactly enough state to make the small PRNG seed space practical.
